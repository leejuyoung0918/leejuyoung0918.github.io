KAIST Global Entrepreneurship Summer School · Team lead

conVRsation

From a need for practice to a reason for institutions to buy
I designed a VR psychological first aid (PFA) training concept informed by interviews with police officers and counselors. I led the team and prepared the product demonstration. Together, we examined public-sector customers, market size and licensing before pitching in English in Silicon Valley.

Period
March – July 2026
My role
Team leadership, field interviews, VR training design and product demonstration
Tools & methods
Customer interviews, PFA scenarios, VR prototyping, B2G business modeling

What interviews revealed about practice

Police officers described the pressure of role-playing in front of colleagues and the difficulty of making it feel real. Counselors wanted more hands-on practice alongside lectures. Professors and researchers emphasized varied crisis situations and ways to evaluate behavior. Our team also spoke with students, counselors and researchers at a psychology conference to refine the need for repeated practice and feedback.

Turning those needs into a product flow

We designed a sequence from choosing a scenario and viewing a person’s profile to Look, Listen and Link, followed by feedback. Look covers the person’s condition and immediate risks. Listen focuses on the conversation and their reactions. Link connects them with support. I walked through this flow using the concept video in our final pitch. We proposed an AI persona that reacts to the trainee’s choices and feedback on their performance.

The trainee and the institutional buyer

The trainees are police officers, counselors and other responders. We identified public mental-health and disaster-response institutions as our initial buyers. To explain the purchasing case, we examined the effort of coordinating role-play actors and workshops. Our B2G model paired repeatable practice with trainee management, usage records and reports.

Our team’s market and revenue model

We started with existing mental-health education and training spending, narrowed it to public PFA and disaster-response training, then considered early-adopter institutions. We proposed annual licenses for both center-level training and oversight across multiple centers.

How we compared alternatives

We examined existing PFA education and VR training products through content fit, localization for Korean settings, repeatable practice, and evaluation and reporting. The combination we proposed linked repeated conversations in local crisis scenarios with records that institutions could use to manage training.

From pilots to institutional adoption

Our proposed rollout began with reviewing product classification and validation requirements for public-sector adoption. R&D-funded pilots would generate evidence, followed by three to five reference centers and expansion to 15–25 centers with a central dashboard. Our follow-up questions covered budget ownership, purchasing decisions and the conditions for converting grant-funded pilots into paid contracts.

Explaining the value in Silicon Valley

I demonstrated the product in English at Plug & Play Tech Center on July 2. Explaining why a responder would practice and why an institution would allocate a budget brought training operations and purchasing into my product decisions. The program selected my reflection, “Dreaming Beyond the Comfort Zone,” for Best Epilogue.
